A demand letter is only as strong as your proof that the landlord received it. Send the best letter in the world by regular mail and you've created a "he said, she said." Send it by certified mail and you've created dated, third-party, government-stamped evidence — the exact thing a small claims judge asks about first.

This is the complete court self-help walkthrough: what to bring, what to say at the counter, what it costs at current 2026 USPS rates, and what goes in your evidence folder if the dispute ends up in front of a judge.

---

## Why Certified Mail — and Why Not Email or Text

When a deposit dispute reaches small claims court, one of the first factual questions is: *did the landlord get the demand, and when?* Here's how each delivery method holds up to that question:

**Regular mail** — no record it was ever sent, received, or when. The landlord simply says "I never got it."

**Email or text** — better than nothing, but landlords claim messages went to spam, an old address, or a phone they no longer use. You're litigating the delivery method instead of the deposit.

**Certified Mail with Return Receipt** — a USPS-stamped receipt proving what you mailed and when, a tracking record showing delivery, and a signature record showing who accepted it. There's nothing left to argue about. Judges see certified mail receipts every day and treat them as the standard.

Certified mail doesn't move faster than regular mail — you're not paying for speed, you're paying for proof.

## Before You Go: The 3-Item Checklist

1. **The signed letter.** Print it, sign it in ink, and make a copy (or scan it) *after* signing — your file copy should show the signature. If you don't have a letter yet, start with a state-specific sample: [Florida](/blog/sample-security-deposit-demand-letter-florida), [Ohio](/blog/ohio-landlord-not-returned-security-deposit-30-days), or [Texas](/blog/sample-security-deposit-demand-letter-texas).
2. **The right address.** Send it to the address where you paid rent or the landlord's official notice address in your lease. If a property manager handled the tenancy, sending a second copy to the owner costs a few extra dollars and closes an escape hatch.
3. **A plain envelope.** Standard #10 business envelope is fine. Write the addresses by hand or print a label — either works.

## At the Post Office, Step by Step

**Step 1 — Ask for "Certified Mail with Return Receipt."** The clerk will hand you two things: **PS Form 3800** (the green-and-white certified mail slip with the tracking number) and, if you choose the physical option, **PS Form 3811** (the green return-receipt card).

**Step 2 — Choose your return receipt type.** You have two options, and this is the only real decision:

- **Electronic Return Receipt ($2.91)** — USPS emails you a PDF with the delivery record and signature. Cheaper, arrives within about a day of delivery, and prints cleanly for a court file. This is the right choice for most people.
- **Green card / PS Form 3811 ($4.65)** — the classic physical card the recipient signs, mailed back to you. Choose it only if you specifically want the physical card; it does the same job for more money and a longer wait.

**Step 3 — Fill out the forms.** PS Form 3800: the landlord's name and address go in the address box. Green card, if you chose it: your name and address on the front (it gets mailed back to you), landlord's on the recipient lines.

**Step 4 — Pay and get your stamped receipt.** The clerk round-stamps your PS Form 3800 with the date. That stamp is your proof of mailing — it's the piece of paper your whole evidence chain hangs on. Do not lose it. Photograph it in the parking lot.

**Step 5 — Save the tracking number** (it's on the PS 3800 receipt, starting with 9407 or similar). You'll watch it at usps.com.

## What It Costs (Current USPS Rates)

USPS rates changed on July 12, 2026. For a standard 1-ounce letter at the counter:

- Certified Mail alone: **$6.37** ($0.82 postage + $5.55 certified fee)
- With Electronic Return Receipt: **$9.28**
- With the physical green card: **$11.02**

Call it **about $9–$11 all-in** — the cost of a sandwich, buying the piece of evidence that most often decides deposit cases. Rates change periodically, so expect small variations.

## Reading the Tracking

Once it's in the system, usps.com tracking will show the letter's journey. Three outcomes matter:

**"Delivered" with a signature** — the gold standard. Screenshot the tracking page and save the return receipt when it arrives. Your deadline clock (the one stated in your letter) runs from here.

**"Refused"** — the landlord declined to accept it. This is not the setback it feels like: you now have a USPS record that the landlord was presented with your demand and turned it away. If the sealed envelope comes back to you, **do not open it** — a returned, still-sealed certified envelope with the tracking history is powerful evidence of exactly what was sent and refused.

**"Unclaimed"** — USPS left notices and the landlord never picked it up. Same principle: keep the returned envelope sealed and the tracking record. A landlord who dodges certified mail rarely looks better for it in court. If this happens, a belt-and-suspenders move is re-sending one copy by regular First-Class mail with a note in your records of the date — the certified attempt plus a mailed copy makes "I never received anything" nearly impossible to sustain.

## The Evidence Folder

If the landlord pays, none of this gets used. If they don't, here's the folder that walks into small claims with you:

1. The signed copy of the demand letter
2. The date-stamped PS Form 3800 receipt (proof of mailing)
3. A printout of the USPS tracking history (proof of delivery, refusal, or avoidance)
4. The return receipt — the PDF or the signed green card (proof of who received it)
5. If it came back: the unopened envelope
6. Your underlying deposit evidence — lease, payment record, move-out photos, and any texts or emails from the landlord

Together, items 1–5 establish the demand beyond argument, which lets the actual hearing be about the only thing you want it to be about: the money.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does it cost to send a demand letter by certified mail?**
At current 2026 rates, about $9.28 with an electronic return receipt or $11.02 with the physical green card, for a standard 1-ounce letter — roughly $9–$11 all-in.

**Should I get the electronic return receipt or the green card?**
Electronic, for most people — it's cheaper, faster, and a PDF prints cleanly for a court file. The green card is the same proof in physical form.

**What if my landlord refuses to sign for it?**
Refusal is itself recorded by USPS, and a documented refusal of a demand letter tends to hurt the refusing party. Keep the returned envelope sealed with the tracking record — and consider mailing one additional copy by regular First-Class mail so no "never received it" argument survives.

**Can I just email the demand letter instead?**
You can send it by email *in addition* — but certified mail is the evidence standard, and some state statutes attach specific legal consequences to certified-mail notices in deposit disputes. Paper first; email as a supplement.

**How long does certified mail take to arrive?**
It travels at First-Class speed — typically within about a week. It's not an express service; the fee buys proof, not speed.

**Certified mail vs. registered mail — which one do I need?**
Certified. Registered mail is a high-security chain-of-custody service for valuables, costs more, and moves slower. For proving delivery of a letter, certified with return receipt is the correct tool.

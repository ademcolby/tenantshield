You moved out weeks ago. The unit was clean, you left a forwarding address, and your landlord has gone quiet. Somewhere in that silence is your money — often more than a thousand dollars — and the question is what to put in writing to get it back.

The document you need is a security deposit refund letter: a written request, sent to your landlord, asking for the return of the deposit and creating a dated record that you asked. Written well, it does more than ask. It supplies the forwarding address many states require before penalties can apply, it fixes the date the landlord was on notice, and it becomes the exhibit a judge reads first if the dispute ever reaches a courtroom.

## Refund Letter, Return Letter, Demand Letter — Which One Do You Need?

These three phrases get used interchangeably online, and they are not the same document. Sorting them out takes thirty seconds and saves you from sending the wrong thing.

A **security deposit refund letter** is sent by the *tenant*, asking for the deposit back. That's almost certainly what you're here for.

A **security deposit return letter** is usually the opposite document — the letter a *landlord* sends a tenant when returning the deposit, often with an itemized list of deductions attached. If you searched that phrase and you're the tenant, you still want the refund letter. If your landlord sent you one of these with deductions you disagree with, the document you need next is a dispute letter, and our guide to [what a security deposit disposition means](/blog/security-deposit-disposition) walks through how to read it.

A **demand letter** is a refund letter with teeth. Same core request, but it cites the statute, states the deadline the landlord has already missed, names the consequence, and gives a firm date for payment. Most tenants who have been ignored once should skip straight to this version. Our [sample security deposit demand letter](/blog/sample-security-deposit-demand-letter) covers it in full.

The practical answer for most people: send one letter, and make it a demand letter. A polite request and a formal demand cost the same stamp, and only one of them does legal work.

## What the Letter Has to Do

A refund letter that only says "please return my deposit" wastes the single best chance you have to shape the dispute. Four jobs matter.

**Supply your forwarding address in writing.** This is the one people skip, and in several states it is the hinge the whole case turns on. In Ohio, the damages and attorney fees under § 5321.16(C) require the tenant to have given a written forwarding address. In Texas, the landlord's obligation is tied to the tenant furnishing one. Your letter can be that written notice — but only if the address is actually in the letter, stated as your forwarding address, not just printed at the top as a return address.

**Fix the date.** A dated letter, sent by a method that proves delivery, establishes when your landlord knew. Statutory clocks and bad-faith presumptions run off that date. Without it you are arguing from memory.

**State the facts plainly.** When you moved out, what condition you left the unit in, what deposit you paid, what you've received back (usually nothing), and whether you owe anything. Short and specific beats long and angry every time.

**Ask for a specific amount by a specific date.** "Return my deposit" is a wish. "Return $1,450 no later than October 3" is a deadline a judge can evaluate.

## Sample Security Deposit Refund Letter

This is a general skeleton. The bracketed parts change by state — and the bracketed parts are exactly where the letter gets its force.

<div class="letter-doc">

[Date]

[Your Name]  
[Your Current Street Address]  
[City, State ZIP]

[Landlord's Name]  
[Landlord's Street Address]  
[City, State ZIP]

**RE: Security Deposit — Written Request for Refund of $[Amount]**

Dear [Mr./Ms. Landlord's Last Name],

I am the former tenant of the residential property located at [Rental Property Address]. In connection with that tenancy, you accepted a security deposit of $[Amount]. My tenancy ended and I delivered possession of the unit to you on [Move-Out Date], leaving it in good condition with only normal wear and tear. I have photographs and a condition checklist documenting the unit's condition at move-out.

To date, you have not returned any portion of my security deposit, and you have provided no written, itemized list of deductions of any kind. I owe no unpaid rent or fees, and there is no basis for withholding any part of the deposit. My forwarding address for all correspondence and for return of the deposit is the address stated above, which I am providing to you in writing.

Under [Your State's Security Deposit Statute, cited by section], a landlord must, within [the statutory period], return the security deposit or provide a written, itemized list of deductions. That period has passed, and you have done neither. [State the specific consequence your state's statute attaches — a forfeiture of the right to withhold, a damages multiplier, or other statutory remedies — citing the section.]

I request refund of the full security deposit of $[Amount]. Please deliver payment to me at my forwarding address stated above no later than [Deadline — typically about 14 days out].

If I do not receive the full amount by that date, I am prepared to pursue all remedies available to me under [Statute], including filing suit in the appropriate court. I would prefer to resolve this matter without litigation, and prompt refund of the deposit will make that unnecessary.

Please direct all correspondence regarding this matter to me at the address above.

Sincerely,

[Your Name]

</div>

## The Brackets Are the Whole Letter

Look at where the blanks sit: the statute, the deadline, and the consequence. Those three are set by state law, and they differ far more than most template sites admit.

In **Florida**, a landlord who fails to send the required notice of claim within 30 days forfeits the right to impose any claim on the deposit under [Florida Statutes § 83.49](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099/0083/Sections/0083.49.html)(3)(a), and the tenant may recover the deposit in full. The tenant's own deadline to object is 15 days.

In **Texas**, a landlord acting in bad faith is liable for a $100 statutory penalty, three times the portion wrongfully withheld, and the tenant's reasonable attorney's fees under § 92.109 — with a 30-day deadline.

In **California**, the deadline is 21 days, and bad-faith retention exposes the landlord to statutory damages of up to twice the deposit under § 1950.5(m), on top of actual damages.

In **Ohio**, the tenant can recover the money due plus damages equal to the amount wrongfully withheld — effectively double — plus reasonable attorney fees under § 5321.16(C), but only where the written forwarding address was given.

Four states, four different deadlines, four different remedies, and one of them turns entirely on a sentence in your letter. A generic refund letter that names none of this is asking politely. A letter that names the right one is doing legal work.

## How to Send It

Send it by a method that proves delivery. In Florida the statute itself runs on certified mail for the landlord's notice, and in every state a certified-mail receipt converts "I sent it" into a fact. Keep a copy of the letter exactly as sent, and keep the receipt with it. Our guide to [sending a demand letter by certified mail](/blog/how-to-send-demand-letter-certified-mail) covers the mechanics and the current costs.

Email alone is rarely enough. Text messages are worse. Neither produces the record a small-claims judge is used to seeing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a security deposit refund letter?**
It is a written request from a tenant to a landlord asking for the return of a security deposit after move-out. A well-drafted one also supplies the tenant's forwarding address in writing, states the move-out date and deposit amount, and sets a firm deadline for payment.

**Is a refund letter the same as a demand letter?**
They overlap. A refund letter asks for the money; a demand letter asks for the money and cites the statute, the missed deadline, and the penalty the landlord is now exposed to. Most tenants whose landlord has already gone silent should send the demand version, since both cost the same to send.

**My landlord sent me a security deposit return letter with deductions. What now?**
That is a different document — the landlord's itemization. Read it against your own move-out photographs and check whether it arrived within your state's deadline, because a late or incomplete itemization can forfeit the landlord's right to withhold anything at all in some states.

**Do I have to send the letter before suing?**
Not usually, but it is close to essential in practice. Many statutory penalties depend on the landlord having received written notice or a forwarding address, and judges expect to see that a tenant asked before filing.

**How long should I give the landlord to respond?**
About 14 days from the date of the letter is the common choice. It is long enough to look reasonable and short enough to keep pressure on.

**What if I never gave a forwarding address?**
Your letter can serve as that written notice. State the address explicitly as your forwarding address for return of the deposit rather than leaving it as a letterhead, since several states condition penalties on it having been furnished in writing.

**Can I still send a letter if I left the unit dirty?**
Yes. A landlord may deduct for actual damage beyond normal wear and tear, but the deduction has to be itemized and justified. A partial dispute is still a dispute, and the deadline rules apply regardless.
